Content Note! this entry discusses a real murder case. Meredith Kercher was a real person, and her family has lived with this case for nearly two decades so she is at the center of the story, not just the trial.

For the fourth stop, I'm taking a quick detour out of Korea to look at one case: the murder of Meredith Kercher in Perugia, Italy, and the trials of American student Amanda Knox. On the surface it's a crime story, but it actually ties together everything I've been writing about all semester: expectations, culture shock, and how we judge people from cultures we don't get. Don't worry, I bring it back to Korea at the end.

The Case

On November 1, 2007, Meredith Kercher, a 21-year-old British exchange student, was murdered in the apartment she shared in Perugia, Italy. Days later, police arrested her roommate Amanda Knox, a 20-year-old exchange student from Seattle, and Knox's Italian boyfriend Raffaele Sollecito. They were basically my age, just college students studying abroad, which is part of why this case hit me.

After a long overnight interrogation, Knox signed statements wrongly accusing her boss, bar owner Patrick Lumumba. He sat in jail for about two weeks until his alibi checked out. Meanwhile, the DNA evidence pointed to Rudy Guede, who was convicted of the murder in a separate trial.

Meredith Kercher

British exchange student, 21. The victim, who honestly got lost behind all the media obsession with Knox.

Amanda Knox

American exchange student, 20. Convicted twice, acquitted twice, and finally cleared of murder for good in 2015.

Raffaele Sollecito

Italian student and Knox's boyfriend. Tried right alongside her, with the same verdicts every time.

Rudy Guede

Convicted of the murder. His sentence got cut down to 16 years, and he was released in 2021.

Patrick Lumumba

Congolese bar owner who Knox falsely accused. Spent about two weeks in jail before he was cleared.

Timeline

The verdict flipped back and forth so many times, and that was one of the most confusing parts of the Italian system for Americans (and for me, trying to keep track of it all).

  1. Nov 1, 2007Meredith Kercher is murdered in Perugia.
  2. Nov 6, 2007Knox, Sollecito, and Lumumba are arrested. Lumumba gets released two weeks later.
  3. Oct 2008Rudy Guede is convicted in a fast-track trial. Guilty
  4. Dec 4, 2009Knox (26 years) and Sollecito (25 years) are convicted of murder. Guilty
  5. Oct 3, 2011Both are acquitted of murder on appeal, and Knox flies home to Seattle. Her slander conviction for accusing Lumumba sticks. Acquitted
  6. Mar 2013Italy's Supreme Court of Cassation throws out the acquittal and orders a new appeal trial. Retrial
  7. Jan 30, 2014A Florence court convicts them again and gives Knox 28½ years, but she stays in the U.S. Guilty
  8. Mar 27, 2015The Court of Cassation clears both of them for good. Exonerated
  9. 2019The European Court of Human Rights rules that Italy violated Knox's rights during her interrogation, since she had no lawyer and no proper interpreter. Rights violated
  10. Jun 2024 – Jan 2025Knox is retried for slander and convicted again, and Italy's top court upholds it in January 2025. She doesn't serve any more time, since she'd already spent almost four years in prison. Slander upheld

How the Legal Systems Differ

The prompt asks about the U.S. and Italy, but since my country is South Korea, I threw in a third column to compare all three.

To a lot of Americans, all the "flip-flopping" looked unfair, like being tried for the same crime over and over. But in Italy, that's just how it works: a trial isn't "done" until every level of appeal is finished. Neither system is automatically better. They just come from different ideas about what protects people most.

Two Countries, Two Amandas

Depending on which country's news you were watching, you got a totally different story.

🇮🇹🇬🇧 In Italian & British tabloids

She was "Foxy Knoxy," a nickname from her old social media that the press turned into the image of a wild, dangerous American girl.

The coverage zoomed in on her love life, how weirdly calm she seemed, and stories like her doing a cartwheel at the police station, all treated like proof she was guilty.

🇺🇸 In American media

She was an innocent, naive girl studying abroad, stuck in a foreign system that wasn't playing fair.

The coverage focused on the weak DNA evidence, the overnight interrogation with no lawyer, and the prosecutor's wild theories.

Both versions turned real people into characters, kind of like a drama plot. And in both, the person who mattered most, Meredith Kercher, ended up as a side character in her own story. Patrick Lumumba, a Black immigrant who was falsely accused, also got way less attention than he deserved.

How Culture Shapes the Way Crime Is Seen

A lot of the suspicion around Knox didn't come from evidence. It came from how she acted: she kissed her boyfriend outside the crime scene, she seemed calm, she did yoga stretches while waiting. People judged all of that based on their own ideas of how a grieving, innocent person is "supposed" to act.

This goes right back to Entry 3, where I realized that something that looks weird from the outside usually makes sense from the inside. People from different cultures, and just different personalities, handle shock and grief in totally different ways. When investigators and reporters judge emotions they don't understand, culture turns into "evidence," and that's scary.

National pride played a part too. A lot of Americans assumed a foreign court had to be biased against an American, while some Italians felt foreign media were attacking their justice system. Everyone was just defending "their own."

Through a Korean Lens

Spending a whole semester on Korea actually changed how I saw this case.

🎬

Juror 8 (2019)

This Korean movie is based on Korea's first-ever citizen jury trial in 2008. Eight regular people have to decide a case that seems obvious, until the defendant takes back his confession. It asks the same question as the Knox case: what happens when a confession isn't what it looks like? It's definitely going on my watch list.

💬

Trial by netizens

In Korea, online communities and "netizens" can turn on someone super fast, and hateful comments (악플, akpeul) have hurt a lot of celebrities. As a K-pop fan, I've seen how fast that can happen. It's the same kind of judging-before-the-verdict that followed Knox around the world.

👀

Nunchi & emotion

Korean culture values reading the room and keeping your emotions in check in public. A Korean student in Knox's shoes could easily have come off as "too calm" to Italian investigators too. Being calm just means different things in different cultures.

Facing the U.S. System as an Outsider

Knox was an American lost in Italy's system, but it's easy to forget it happens the other way around too. Imagine a Korean exchange student at my school, CSUN, getting caught up in a criminal case here in LA:

Language

Even with a court interpreter, legal English is confusing (honestly, it's confusing for me too). Knox's case shows how one bad translation during questioning can change everything.

Unfamiliar rights

Everyone knows "You have the right to remain silent" from TV, but someone from another country might not realize how important it is to ask for a lawyer right away.

Plea bargains

Most U.S. criminal cases end in plea deals, not trials. Korea doesn't have formal plea bargaining like this, so it could feel like pressure to just admit guilt.

A jury of strangers

In Korea, professional judges usually make the call. Getting judged by 12 random people, who might have their own biases about foreigners, could feel really unfamiliar.

Bail & consulates

Cash bail isn't common in a lot of countries. Foreign nationals also have the right to contact their consulate, but they might not even know to ask.

End of the Line

This whole journal started because of a Wonder Girls song my mom used to play and a YouTube recommendation that led me to BTS. Along the way, I realized how much of my picture of Korea came from a screen (Entry 2), and that every cultural difference has a history behind it (Entry 3). The Amanda Knox case shows what happens when people skip those lessons: expectations replace facts, people from one culture judge behavior from another, and the media turns everyone into a character.

Cross-cultural understanding isn't just about loving another country's food or music. It's about slowing down before we judge someone whose "normal" is different from ours, whether we're travelers, jurors, reporters, or just people scrolling online.

I came into this class as a K-pop and K-drama fan. I'm leaving with a way better understanding of Korea, and of how important it is to see people in context before deciding who they are. 감사합니다 for riding along with me.
